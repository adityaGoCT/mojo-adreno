# GPU or NPU on Snapdragon: speaker notes

## 1. GPU or NPU? What we measured on Snapdragon

This deck puts side by side what we measured on two kinds of Snapdragon hardware. The GPU is the Adreno: the 840 in the phones, the X2-90 in the X2 Elite laptop, running our Mojo kernels. The NPU is the Hexagon’s HTP, version V81 in both, which we reach through ONNX Runtime’s QNN execution provider with fp16 graphs. Three parts. First, a mixture-of-experts model, Qwen3-30B-A3B, on the laptop’s GPU and its NPU: there the GPU wins, because the NPU’s static graphs compute all 128 experts instead of the 8 a token chose, sixteen times the useful work. Second, where the NPU does help: prefilling dense models. Llama 3.2 1B’s 431-token prompt takes 168 milliseconds on the HTP against 685 on the GPU, 4.1 times faster, and π0’s Gemma 2B prefix 0.80 seconds against 2.34. Third, Gemma 4 E2B and E4B on the phone’s GPU: 28.1 milliseconds a token for E2B in int4, and a new int8 storage that brings its choices to the bf16 model’s. Every number comes from the repo’s READMEs or the sessions’ notes, and each slide names its source.

## 2. Two ways onto the chip

Two routes onto the same chip. On the GPU, everything is Mojo: DeviceContext programs whose kernels the fork’s adreno840 target compiles to SPIR-V, run through Vulkan, on the phone’s Adreno 840 and the laptop’s X2-90. Because the kernels run whatever the host enqueues, they can make decisions at run time: an expert kernel reads the ids the router wrote, and a prompt can be any length. Weights are int4 or int8, unpacked inside the kernels, and prefill uses the cooperative-matrix units. The NPU route is different. A retail phone has no Qualcomm SDK, but ONNX Runtime’s QNN execution provider and QNN’s HTP libraries are public on Maven Central. We write fp16 ONNX graphs, and the phone compiles them for the HTP once, for fixed shapes: Llama’s graphs take 448 rows and 201 seconds to compile; π0’s take 832 rows and 18.5 minutes. Each process then loads the compiled contexts, about 2 seconds for Llama. Quantized weights work only per output channel, as we will see. The two meet in a handoff: the NPU prefills the prompt and the GPU decodes from its K/V cache. Each part’s K/V is copied while the NPU runs the next part, so only 0.8 to 2 milliseconds remain after the last one.

## 3. An MoE: dynamic on the GPU, static on the NPU

Qwen3-30B-A3B is a mixture of experts: 48 layers, each with 128 experts, of which a router picks 8 per token. That choice depends on the token, and it is made on the device. On the GPU that is easy. The router’s top 8 ids and weights stay in GPU memory, and the expert kernels read them and find their int4 rows, so a token reads only 1.63 gigabytes of weights. In prefill, the tokens are grouped by expert, about 25 tokens per expert for a 404-token prompt, and each expert runs once over its tokens, reading its weights once: a grouped GEMM. That reads a 404-token prompt in 4.24 to 4.31 seconds instead of 11.8 to 13.1 a token at a time. On the NPU, the graph is static. It cannot select weights by an index it computed, so the router’s top 8 become a dense weight per token, and the graph runs all 128 experts for every token and weights their outputs. That is sixteen times the useful arithmetic. And the model would not fit anyway: in fp16 it is 61 gigabytes against the laptop’s 33.6, so on the HTP it would need quantized weights.

## 4. Qwen3-30B-A3B: the GPU wins both phases

Here are the measurements. We ran one real layer of Qwen3-30B-A3B, layer 0 in fp16, on the laptop’s HTP: attention, the router and all 128 experts. A decode token takes 10.7 to 14 milliseconds through that one layer; scaled to 48 layers, half a second or more per token, against 27 to 32 milliseconds for the whole token on the GPU in int4. A 256-token prefill takes 95 milliseconds a layer, 4.6 seconds for 48, against 2.6 seconds on the GPU. At 128 tokens QNN compiled a worse plan, 213 milliseconds a layer. The 48-layer column is the README’s scaling of the one-layer time, not a run of the whole model, which would not fit in fp16. The interesting part is that the hardware itself is fast: it streams fp16 weights at about 100 gigabytes a second, faster than our GPU kernels’ 55 to 60, and does 3.4 teraflops of fp16 matrix work. What loses is the static graph, which must move all 128 experts’ weights. The README estimates that reading only the 8 chosen ones would take about 0.8 milliseconds a layer. The layer is also accurate, once the QK norms are written as L2 normalizations. So for this model the GPU does both phases, and the NPU becomes interesting when its graphs can gather by index.

## 5. Llama 3.2 1B: the NPU prefills 4.1× faster, decode ties

Llama 3.2 1B is where the NPU shines. The prompt is a 431-token passage, prefilled on the S26 Ultra’s HTP in fp16, and decoded either on the NPU or by our Mojo program on the Adreno. The HTP prefills in 169 milliseconds, against 685 on the GPU in fp16 and 641 in 4-bit: 4.1 and 3.8 times faster, 2,550 tokens a second. Prefill is large matrix products over fixed shapes, exactly what the HTP is built for. Decode is different: a token reads all 2.47 gigabytes of fp16 weights, and both processors run at about the same memory speed, 63 gigabytes a second, so all-NPU and all-GPU decode tie at 38.8 and 39.8 milliseconds. With 4-bit weights, the GPU decodes three times faster, 13.2 milliseconds; the NPU has no 4-bit row because int4 on the HTP needs quantized activations. So the best split here is NPU prefill and GPU decode, with a handoff of about a millisecond left after the NPU. The one token difference in the 4-bit row is a near-tie at token 38, flipped by the fp16 prefill; decoding from the NPU’s own K/V in PyTorch gives exactly the phone’s 80 tokens.

## 6. Quantized weights on the NPU: per channel, not blocks

Can quantized weights make the NPU faster still? Partly. With weights quantized per output channel and 16-bit activations, Llama’s prefill drops from 168 to 116 milliseconds with int8, and 111 with int4: 1.45 and 1.5 times faster than fp16, and about six times the Adreno. But accuracy suffers. The w8a16 graph’s K/V are within 3 percent of the fp16 model’s, and the text stays coherent but becomes a paraphrase after 29 tokens. Int4 per channel is too coarse for Llama. The accurate int4 form, with a scale per block of 64, only reaches QNN as low-power block quantization, and on this stack that is slow as a MatMul, 495 milliseconds, and crashes QNN as a FullyConnected for every shape but 2048 by 2048. The ternary Qwen3-8B fits the per-channel path better: its weights are already coarse, and with int4 per channel on the HTP its 408-token prompt prefills in 1.37 seconds against 3.60 on the GPU, 2.6 times faster, and all 80 generated tokens match the GPU’s exact-ternary run. These results are on the npu-quant branch, not yet merged.

## 7. π0: Gemma 2B’s prefix on the NPU, the rest on the GPU

π0, the robot policy, has the same shape as a language model’s prefill plus decode. A SigLIP encoder reads three camera images, Gemma 2B runs once over the 816-row prefix of image and language tokens, and a smaller expert denoises a 50 by 32 action chunk in 10 steps against the prefix’s K/V cache. The prefix is dense matrix work over a fixed shape, so it moves to the NPU: six fp16 graphs of three layers each, compiled once. On the Galaxy Z Fold it takes 0.80 seconds instead of 2.34, 2.9 times faster, and the chunk goes from 3.6 to 2.05 to 2.16 seconds. On the X2 Elite laptop, where the Vulkan server itself runs the NPU and writes the K/V straight into the GPU’s buffers, the prefix takes 0.81 seconds against 2.13 to 2.15, and the chunk 1.82 to 1.85 seconds against 3.1 to 3.9. The actions move from 1.1e-5 to 3.2e-3 of PyTorch’s fp32 model; all of that comes from the fp16 prefix, because PyTorch’s own steps from the handed-off K/V match the device within 1.4e-6. And the GPU no longer holds Gemma 2B at all: 1.4 gigabytes instead of 5.4.

## 8. Where the NPU helps, and where it does not

Here is everything on one table. The NPU wins wherever the work is a large dense prefill over a fixed shape: Llama’s prompt 4.1 times faster in fp16, 5.9 times with int8 weights at a cost in accuracy, the ternary Qwen3-8B’s prompt 2.6 times, and π0’s Gemma prefix 2.9 times on the phone and 2.6 on the laptop. Decode is a memory-bound loop over all the weights, and in fp16 the two processors tie, at about 63 gigabytes a second. With 4-bit weights the GPU decodes three times faster, and the NPU would need quantized graphs. And the mixture of experts goes to the GPU in both phases, because the NPU’s static graph must compute every expert; those two NPU numbers are one measured layer times 48. SmolVLA is the counter-example of a different kind: its prefix is only 62 milliseconds of a 776-millisecond chunk on the GPU, so we did not move it to the NPU at all; there is no measurement for it there.

## 9. Gemma 4 E2B and E4B on the Adreno 840, int4

The third part is Gemma 4, Google’s new on-device models, on the Adreno 840 alone. Both E2B and E4B run as Mojo programs on a Galaxy Z Fold, with int4 weights: 1.21 and 2.47 gigabytes on the GPU. E2B decodes a token in 28.1 milliseconds, E4B in 53.8. Decode reads every weight each token, at 43 and 46 gigabytes a second. A 960-token prompt is prefilled in batches on the cooperative-matrix units in 2.36 and 4.71 seconds, 13 and 15 times faster than a token at a time; the int4 projections are most of that time, at about 2 teraflops. Gemma 4 has new structure: its later layers reuse earlier layers’ K/V caches, and every layer takes a per-layer input from a second embedding table, 4.7 gigabytes for E2B, which stays in the checkpoint file while the program reads one row per token. The phone’s logits are within 1.7e-4 of transformers’ own Gemma 4 layers on the same int4 weights, and every greedy token matches. But against the original bf16 models, the greedy choice agrees at only 39 to 55 of 48 to 64 positions. That gap is int4 rounding, spread over every kind of matrix, which is what the next slide fixes.

## 10. int8 closes the gap to bf16, at 1.5× the decode time

The gemma4 branch now has two more storages. int8 stores every matrix but the output head in 8 bits, with an fp16 scale per 64 weights like int4’s. With it both models make the bf16 models’ greedy choice at every position of the long prompt and all but one of the short one, and the mean KL divergence drops 25 to 130 times, to between 0.003 and 0.012. The storage was chosen by simulation first: a finer int4 grid barely helps, while 8 bits nearly close the gap; and every part of the model costs about the same in int4 except the head, which stays int4 to save a fifth of a gigabyte per token. Mixed also leaves the MLPs of the K/V-sharing layers in int4, and lands in between: good on the long prompt, less so on the short one. The cost is decode time: int8 reads 1.8 times the bytes and takes about 1.5 times as long, 41.1 milliseconds for E2B and 83.0 for E4B; mixed takes 33.1 and 73.0. Prefill hardly changes, 2 to 4 percent, because the matrix products, not the weight reads, set its time. These times come from one series on a second Fold, where int4 matched the first phone within a percent.

## 11. What decides GPU or NPU

So what decides where a model runs? Dense prefill over a fixed shape goes to the NPU: 2.6 to 4.1 times faster for Llama, the ternary Qwen3-8B and π0’s prefix, and when the handoff of the K/V cache overlaps the NPU’s work, only 0.8 to 2 milliseconds of it remain for Llama, and 2.4 to 3.3 for π0 on the laptop. Decode is memory-bound: in fp16 the two tie, and with int4 weights the GPU is three times faster, because int4 on the HTP needs quantized graphs. Anything decided at run time stays on the GPU, above all a mixture of experts, whose routing a static graph cannot follow. What comes next follows from that. On the NPU: graphs that gather experts by index, or the NPU taking an MoE’s dense attention; chunked graphs that take a K/V input, so any prompt length runs without recompiling; and Gemma 4’s prefill on the HTP, the way π0’s prefix runs there. On the GPU: something between int4 and int8 for Gemma 4, either 5 or 6 bits or better int4 rounding for the matrices the mixed storage leaves in int4.
